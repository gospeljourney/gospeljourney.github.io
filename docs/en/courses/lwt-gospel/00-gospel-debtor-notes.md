# Debtor to the Gospel — Lecture Notes

> **A note on the audio:** This lesson's audio is a sermon recording in Korean. The notes below are in English, but pressing play will play Korean speech.

## 1. The Church in Rome

<AudioCue t="0:00" note="#_1-the-church-in-rome" />

Romans 1:8-15 carries Paul's deep longing to visit Rome. The lecture notes that this passage comes before Romans turns to its main argument — more like an introduction — but that it still shows something important about Paul's heart.

The distance from Corinth, where Paul is thought to have written this letter, to Rome was about 1,300 kilometers by land and sea combined — more than a month's journey even by horse and ship. Yet Paul longed to visit Rome, a city he had never seen. The lecture explains that in the early days of Christianity, the church had not yet been planted throughout the world, but word had reached Paul that someone had preached the gospel in Rome — the center of the world at that time — and that people there had received it.

### Rejoicing in the Faith of the Roman Church

<AudioCue t="1:21" />

Paul had heard that the believers in Rome had not only received the gospel but were living out their faith well. The church in Rome was not one Paul had preached or founded, but that news alone brought him joy and made him thank God. Romans 1:8 is mentioned alongside this confession of thanks.

The lecture explains that here Paul carried the heart of God the Father. Even in a place he had no direct connection to, he could rejoice and give thanks that God's people were standing firm in faith. And so Paul came to long to visit Rome.

### Praying for a Church He Had Never Seen

<AudioCue t="2:30" />

In Romans 1:9-10, Paul prays without ceasing for the Roman church he has never seen, asking that a good way to reach them might open up within God's will. The lecture says Paul's prayer had a wide scope. Since it was not humanly easy for him to reach Rome, Paul prayed asking for God's favor to open a door for him to go.

### A Shepherd's Heart to Build Them Up

<AudioCue t="3:40" />

In Romans 1:11, Paul says the reason he wants to see the Roman church is to share a spiritual gift with them so that they may be established. The lecture explains that the New Testament had not yet been completed at that time, and there were no church buildings like we have today. The gospel had been preached, and people had begun gathering to serve God through the work of the Holy Spirit, but in Paul's eyes they were still in a fragile state.

The lecture compares this to a newborn baby — beautiful and precious, but easily shaken by trial and hardship. Paul wanted to share the power of the word entrusted to him, and the grace and gifts of the Spirit, and to help them take deeper root and stand unshaken through prayer and worship together. The lecture describes this heart as the heart of God the Father, and the heart of a shepherd.

The speaker shares that when he sees a soul who wants to walk with God but doesn't know how, he feels an urgent longing to help. He describes wanting to give whatever is in him so that struggling believers can encounter the Lord in worship, receive grace, and stand firm in faith. He also mentions an example of a mentor who wisely taught someone a prayer that helped them break through a difficulty.

### Koinonia — Being Encouraged Through One Another's Faith

<AudioCue t="8:14" />

Romans 1:12 introduces Paul's desire that he and the Roman church might encourage one another through their faith. The lecture calls this *koinonia*. It explains that koinonia is not merely human friendship or fellowship in a natural, social sense, but a spiritual exchange among people who have been born again — people who serve the one God the Father, live in the one Holy Spirit, and share in the one word.

The lecture says that the Spirit who lives in me also lives in the brother or sister in front of me, and in the brothers and sisters of the Roman church far away. Because everyone is walking toward the same heaven, a spiritual exchange takes place when we share the one word in the one Spirit. At that point, one person's confession of the grace they received becomes grace for someone else, and when people join hands and pray together, the Spirit moves among them and brings restoration and healing.

The lecture points to small group and workplace fellowship meetings as places where this same koinonia of faith can be experienced. Even when believers belong to different churches, they can draw strength from worshiping together in the one Spirit. It also mentions experiences on the mission field, where people who share no common language still find joy and encouragement worshiping and praying together in the Spirit. Providing a vehicle to support a missionary's work, and the joy that missionary felt because of that help, is mentioned as another example of the joy that comes to those who serve together in this way.

The lecture also describes partnering with and serving a church at home and a church abroad as another form of this mutual encouragement through faith — a way through which God's grace and gifts can be poured out.

### Reflection Questions

Do I have a heart, like Paul's, that rejoices and prays for the faith of people I have never met in person, and that longs to help the weak stand firm in faith?

## 2. Bearing Fruit

<AudioCue t="13:16" note="#_2-bearing-fruit" />

In Romans 1:13, Paul says he wants to bear fruit even among the Roman church. The lecture explains that bearing fruit was the life goal Paul carried after he met the Lord.

### The Meaning of Fruit

<AudioCue t="13:16" />

The lecture says the fruit spoken of here does not mean evangelism alone, since the Roman church had already met the Lord. Evangelism is fruit too, but the lecture explains that it is also fruit when someone who has already received the gospel is built up further, spiritually, through the gifts and graces given to them.

As in the mission example mentioned earlier, when someone channels resources to help a worker carry out their mission well and supports the work so that it goes forward, that too is, before God, part of a larger and more complete fruit. The lecture says that when one church serves another and helps it stand firm, those who gave through finances, prayer, and physical service also share in the reward for that fruit.

### The Purpose the Lord Established, and Paul's Longing

<AudioCue t="14:36" />

The lecture explains that John 15:16 speaks of the purpose for which the Lord established us: to bear fruit. Paul wanted to pour out everything — the grace, power, and gifts given to him — to bear fruit wherever he went, for God's glory.

The lecture says that Paul considered it fruit before God if he could travel all the way to Rome, share the grace and gospel entrusted to him, and see their faith made firm. It says Paul believed God would reward him when he came before God having borne fruit, and confessed his own desire to bear fruit.

### Using the Gifts and Resources We Have Been Given

<AudioCue t="15:42" />

The lecture says that bearing fruit should not be Paul's life purpose alone but the life purpose of every Christian. It explains that once a person is saved, they are given a mission to bear fruit for God, and that God has given each person gifts, talents, and resources for that purpose.

The lecture says that what a person accumulates, and even the body itself, can never truly belong to that person forever. That is why it matters that someone who is saved uses the gifts, grace, and resources God has given for the sake of God's kingdom. When we use them this way, God's life flows, the weak are built up, the poor are strengthened, and souls are saved.

### Crowns, Rewards, and the One Without Fruit

<AudioCue t="16:39" />

The lecture says that when a person stands before the Lord, God will remove every veil and show the results borne out in that person's life. It explains that God will say, "Well done, my good and faithful servant," to the one who bore fruit and gave God glory, and will give that person a crown.

The lecture then uses language of warning as well. It says that if someone buries the grace and gifts God gave them in the ground, uses what they have only for themselves, and does nothing for the kingdom of God, they become someone without fruit. The lecture states, "One without fruit does not enter heaven," and explains that the evidence of salvation lies in whether a person is bearing fruit. This is recorded here as a claim made in the lecture, not as a statement of fact in this summary.

Paul had already borne the fruit of many churches being planted, but he wanted to bear still more fruit and give God glory. The lecture considers Paul's desire to go to Rome, and his longing to meet them and share the gifts given to him, in this light. It says that although this introductory section comes before the main body of the gospel message, we still need to learn the attitude and mindset with which Paul lived.

### Reflection Questions

Where am I using the gifts, talents, and resources God has given me? Do I have the longing for fruit that Paul carried — and if not, can I ask God to restore that heart in me?

## 3. Debtor to the Gospel

<AudioCue t="17:51" note="#_3-debtor-to-the-gospel" />

In Romans 1:14-15, Paul confesses that he is a debtor to Greeks and to barbarians, to the wise and to the foolish alike, and says he wants to preach the gospel to the people in Rome as well. The lecture explains one of Paul's motives for wanting to preach the gospel and go to Rome as a heart bent on repaying a debt.

### A Debtor to Everyone

<AudioCue t="19:03" />

The lecture describes the Greeks as the advanced citizens at the center of the world at that time, and the barbarians as the various peoples considered uncivilized. From the wise to the foolish, Paul says he is a debtor to everyone. This may be hard to grasp at first, but the lecture explains that Paul was moved to act because he wanted to repay a debt. His desire to go to Rome and preach the gospel came from that same debt-repaying heart.

### "Compelled" and "Debtor": A Korean Phonetic Rendering Left in the Transcript

<AudioCue t="20:29" />

1 Corinthians 9:16 is brought in alongside this. The lecture explains that the word rendered "compelled" in that verse and the phrase "in debt" in Romans 1:14 share the same root. The automatic transcription renders "compelled" as "opeil," "in debt" as "opeio," and a related noun form as "opei," and explains that these share a common root.

This Korean phonetic rendering is only what survived in the automatic transcription. The original-language spelling, or the exact linguistic form behind it, cannot be confirmed from this recording alone. These notes therefore record the lecture's explanation using this phonetic rendering as given, without reconstructing a spelling or asserting the original-language form as settled fact.

Building on this explanation, the lecture frames Paul's reason for preaching the gospel as being because he is in debt. Just as failing to repay a debt brings demands or legal consequences, Paul treated preaching the gospel and going to Rome as a debt he had to repay.

### Paul, Who Counted Grace as a Debt

<AudioCue t="21:42" />

The lecture explains that although Paul had once persecuted the church and hated Jesus, he met the Lord through God's favor and free grace, received the laying on of hands from Ananias, and was saved through receiving the Holy Spirit. Paul counted the grace he received as a debt. The lecture puts it this way: it was written as grace, but read as debt — and Paul received it as a debt of love.

The lecture explains that although Paul was someone who could not have deserved grace, the foremost of sinners, God gave him grace anyway — and so Paul carried the resolve to repay this debt even at the cost of his life.

### The Revelation in the Arabian Desert, and the Debt of the Gospel

<AudioCue t="22:57" />

The lecture says that God gave Paul an especially great revelation of the gospel. The speaker mentions that thirteen books of the New Testament were written through Paul, and explains that the pattern of faith for the Gentile world, salvation by faith, and a life of freedom lived in the Spirit were all recorded through Paul's letters.

The lecture says that God powerfully revealed this gospel to Paul while he was in the Arabian desert. It explains that God opened up to Paul the new framework — the new wineskin — needed for the transition from the first covenant of the Old Testament to the new covenant of the New Testament. Paul, who had once persecuted the church himself, counted it a debt that God had shown him immeasurable grace and revealed to him this great gospel.

And so Paul believed he had to repay that debt by preaching this gospel to everyone — to Greeks and barbarians, to the wise and to the foolish. The lecture says that Paul carried the resolve to travel the 1,300 kilometers by ship and on foot even through storm and wave, and that even if he were imprisoned, he would still write letters though his body was bound, and that he preached the gospel with this heart. The lecture mentions Ephesians, Colossians, and Philippians as letters written from prison.

### Suffering and the Heart of a Drink Offering

<AudioCue t="25:21" />

The lecture says that the gratitude a person feels when they first receive grace can fade with time. It calls us to examine whether we have come to regard God as the one in debt to us instead of ourselves as debtors, and to notice when we grow resentful in the face of hardship. But living faithfully does not mean everything goes smoothly, and Paul himself suffered hardship for preaching the gospel.

Regarding Philippians 1:29, the lecture explains that the grace God gives is not only about believing, being blessed, and rejoicing, but also about sharing in suffering for the sake of the Lord's kingdom. Paul was thankful even in suffering, and — without anyone telling him to — prayed with joy for those who had met the Lord, longing to share with them the grace, gifts, and gospel given to him. Because he was a debtor, he wanted to repay that debt even at the cost of his life.

<AudioCue t="27:18" />

The lecture explains Paul's final confession that he was willing even to be poured out as a drink offering. A drink offering, the lecture explains by way of comparison, was, in Old Testament sacrifice, the pouring of new wine or strong drink onto a sacrifice as it burned low, to keep the fire alive. Paul was willing to offer his own blood as a martyr's drink offering if it meant reviving the faith of others and rebuilding the church.

The lecture says that Paul lived with this heart, and that God, through Paul's devotion, raised up many churches across Asia and Europe in a short time. The lecture explains that the reason Paul bore so much fruit was that the Lord was pleased with this devotion.

### A Testimony from a 1992 Prayer Retreat

<AudioCue t="28:40" />

The speaker shares a personal testimony about going to a prayer retreat with students for a winter retreat in January 1992. He recalls that a cross stood on the wall there, with a painting of Jesus looking up at the cross beneath it. A worship team led songs at the front, and the speaker says he was deeply moved as he listened, especially by the lyrics about living one's whole life as a debtor and repaying the Lord's debt.

The speaker says he was twenty-two years old at the time and could not grasp the full meaning of every line, but says that when the Holy Spirit opens a heart, God's heart and will can pierce a soul regardless of age. He describes weeping, offering himself to God, and confessing that he would repay this debt by preaching the gospel and the word. (The transcript contains an unclear phrase connected to his sense of calling at this point, and that sentence has not been filled in.)

<AudioCue t="31:57" />

The speaker recalls walking for a time afterward, singing that song and weeping. He says this was the same heart that moved Paul, and he asks the audience whether they, too, see themselves as debtors. Thinking of a world where some people have still never once heard the gospel in their whole lives, the lecture says that Paul would have sold his possessions if that is what it took to go and preach the gospel.

### Recovering a Lost Heart

<AudioCue t="33:23" />

The lecture says that when this heart is lost and fades, complacency and resentment creep in. It warns against a life of faith that resents God as though God had become the debtor and we the creditor, and prays that God would restore a humble heart and the heart Paul carried.

### Reflection Questions

In what spirit am I receiving God's grace? Can I ask God to restore in me the heart of a debtor to the gospel that Paul carried?
